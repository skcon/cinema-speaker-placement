# Doors, windows and furniture: what they do to placement and the estimate

Researched 2026-10-15 for issue #43 (research: doors, windows and furniture in speaker placement). It is the evidence for the room-objects ADR (#45), which decides what the app models, what it only warns about and what it only draws. It builds on `low-frequency-response.md` (room modes) and `crossover-enclosure-boundaries.md` (boundary interference), and uses the terms in `GLOSSARY.md`.

**How it was read.** During this session the web fetcher couldn't resolve any host, and direct downloads (dolby.com among them) were refused by the network proxy. So **every web source below was read only through search-engine summaries** of its text. None was opened in full, and no page numbers are cited. The textbooks were **not re-read**. They are cited by chapter, for standard results, as `low-frequency-response.md` does. Treat every claim here as **UNVERIFIED against the full text** unless its row says otherwise. Section 7 lists what to re-read once fetching works.

## Conventions

- **Opening**: a door, a doorway or a window in one of the room's four walls. It has a wall, a position along that wall, a width and a height, and a state (closed or open). This is a working term for #45, not yet in `GLOSSARY.md`.
- **Furniture box**: an axis-aligned box on the floor (width, depth, height). The couch is the Seat row from `GLOSSARY.md`.
- **Primary**: textbooks, standards (ITU, EBU, CEDIA/CTA), peer-reviewed or conference papers, and manufacturers' own technical documents. **Community practice**: forum posts and blogs. Community sources are labelled as such in the text and are never the only source for a number.
- **Ours**: an inference, threshold or calculation made for this note, not taken from a source.
- **UNVERIFIED**: a claim we could not check against its source's own text.

## 2. Openings: what a door or a window does

### 2.1 Low frequencies (about 20–200 Hz, the room-mode range)

1. **The rigid-wall model assumes no openings.** The app's mode frequencies and shapes come from the rigid-walled rectangular room (KUTTRUFF ch. 3; `low-frequency-response.md` §2). Walls that aren't rigid (finite admittance) shift the modes slightly and give each mode its own damping (KUTTRUFF §3.4, by section title; not re-read).
2. **An open opening is not a perfect absorber at low frequencies.** Sabine's convention treats an open window as absorption coefficient α = 1. ODEON-2025 models the window as an absorbing area in a rigid baffle with a radiation impedance. It finds that α depends on the angle of incidence and **falls below 1 toward low frequencies**, which it puts down to edge scattering (search summary only). We found **no published α for a door-sized opening at 20–200 Hz**.
3. **Which modes an opening damps.** Absorption on one wall mainly damps the modes that have a non-zero index along that wall's axis. Modes that only graze the wall are damped much less. Hunt et al. (1939) showed this, as MAGALOTTI reports it (secondary; Hunt not read). So a door in a side wall damps the width modes most and the length and height axial modes hardly at all.
4. **An open door couples the room to its neighbour.** The second room's own resonances and the filtering by the doorway make coupled rooms hard to model (COUPLED-ARXIV, from measurements with the door at two angles). Their decay is double-sloped, not single-exponential (secondary overview, **UNVERIFIED**). A closed-form model of the combined system needs the adjacent room's geometry, which the app doesn't have.
5. **How big is the effect against what the app already assumes?** This calculation is **ours**. With uniform absorption, Sabine's formula and δ = 3·ln10/T (`low-frequency-response.md` §2.5) give δ = c·A/(8V), with A the total absorption area. Take a 6 × 4.5 × 2.6 m room (V ≈ 70 m³) at the app's assumed T = 0.5 s: A ≈ 0.161·V/T ≈ 23 m². An open door of 0.9 × 2.0 m at α = 1 (an upper bound, see item 2) adds 1.8 m². That is about 8% more damping overall, or roughly double that for the modes along the door wall's axis (item 3). It is the same order as the uncertainty in our assumed T = 0.5 s, which no source supports for "a home theater". An open-plan side (a whole wall missing) is different. That is not a rectangular room at all, and it is outside ADR 0002.
6. **Closed doors and windows.** Glass, doors and wall panels are flexible plates that absorb some low-frequency energy (diaphragmatic or panel absorption; MHA "Absorption" chapter, by title; not re-read). Glass absorbs more at low frequencies than at high ones (a university course page via search summary, secondary). We found no verified 125 Hz value for window glass in a primary table: a forum post gives about 0.3 (**community practice**, **UNVERIFIED**).
7. **Furniture hardly matters here.** Room dimensions affected the low-frequency field in dwellings more than furniture or deviations from a rectangle did (MALUSKI-2004, as a citing paper summarizes it; the search summary of the paper says furniture had little effect below 100 Hz, **UNVERIFIED**).
8. **Community practice**: home-theater posters report less bass with an open doorway, describe the doorway as "enlarging the enclosure", recommend a closed door for the best bass, and report poor results for a subwoofer placed near a wall opening (C-AH-DOOR). They also recommend measuring with the door open and closed instead of calculating (C-GS-GLASS, coupled-room thread). These are anecdotes, not measurements.

### 2.2 Higher frequencies (above about 200 Hz: reflections and imaging)

1. **A missing or different reflector.** Above a few hundred hertz an open opening returns little of the sound that hits it (ODEON-2025: α near 1 once the opening is large against the wavelength). A closed window reflects mid and high frequencies like a hard wall, while letting more bass through (**community practice**, C-GS-GLASS, C-SN-WINDOW; consistent with item 2.1.6).
2. **Wavelength limits.** An opening or an object only acts as a "hole" or a "shadow" when it is large compared with the wavelength (general diffraction physics, MHA "Diffraction" chapter by title). A 0.9 m door matches the wavelength at c/0.9 ≈ 380 Hz (**ours**). So across much of the app's 80–500 Hz boundary-interference range a doorway is not yet a clean hole.
3. **Left/right asymmetry.** Multichannel listening rooms should place the main loudspeakers symmetrically about the line from the listening position to the centre speaker (EBU-3276-S1). Genelec says reflective objects near the monitors should be placed symmetrically to keep the stereo image (GEN-1237A). Linkwitz counts non-symmetrical placement among the practices that harm reproduction (LINKWITZ-EDN, secondary). Forum readers of TOOLE-SR report that he treats side-wall reflections as mostly harmless or useful, rather than something to remove (**UNVERIFIED**, community quotes only). That supports *matching* the two sides, not eliminating reflections. Forum posters with one open side say: don't over-absorb the closed side, aim L/R at the far seats, treat the first reflection points, and let room correction do the rest (**community practice**, C-AVS-OPEN).

**Which effects matter for placement advice.** At low frequencies an opening mainly matters through **where the subwoofers sit relative to it**, and through coupling the app can't model. The damping it adds is small compared with our existing assumptions (item 2.1.5). At higher frequencies the useful effect is **asymmetry**: a door or window at one speaker's first side reflection point and a wall at the other's. Both are easier to turn into warnings than into a model.

## 4. Furniture

1. **Blocking the direct sound.** "The propagation of the direct sound should not be affected by any obstacles between the loudspeakers and the listening area (this is not so important at the lowest frequencies, below 150 Hz)" (EBU-3276, 1998 edition, quoted in a search summary). Genelec says not to put monitors behind objects (GEN-8030A). Why it matters: an object large compared with the wavelength shadows the direct sound, and sound gets past only by diffraction (MHA "Diffraction" chapter by title; general physics). So the rule is about mid and high frequencies, and **subwoofers are exempt** (EBU-3276's 150 Hz note).
2. **Near-boundary furniture: desks, consoles, cabinets.** Reflections off desks, cabinets and computer screens colour the sound. Keep monitors and the listening position clear of reflective surfaces, and leave at least 3 cm clearance behind, above and beside a monitor (GEN-8430A). Monitors on stands behind and above a desk usually sound better than monitors on the desk (Genelec M030/M040 guide, via the same search; secondary copy). Placing a monitor horizontally can strengthen early reflections from the desk (GEN-8030A).
3. **A speaker inside a cabinet or shelf cavity.** **No source found.** Searches turned up only general enclosure-resonance material and forum talk.
4. **Couch against the back wall.**
   - Every length (front-to-back) axial mode has a pressure maximum at the back wall (mode shape, KUTTRUFF ch. 3). So a seat against the wall hears every length mode at full strength and sits on no length-mode null. The app's room-mode model **already shows this** at the MLP, because the MLP's y position is an input to the mode shapes.
   - **Community practice**: ears close to the back wall get a bass boost, like a speaker near a wall. The figure given is about +6 dB below about 140 Hz at 0.3 m (C-OHM-REAR, a manufacturer marketing blog; **UNVERIFIED**, no primary source, so no number should be used). Posters note the trade-off: deeper bass against a strong, early reflection from behind.
   - The usual starting point, the 38% seat position, is an informal heuristic (`dolby-speaker-placement.md` §8, item 3).
5. **Rows of seats (seat-dip effect).** Over many rows of seats in a concert hall, grazing sound loses 15–20 dB at around 150 Hz. The loss builds up over about 12 rows and depends on seat height (SCHULTZ-1964, abstract). It needs many rows and grazing incidence, so it is outside v1, which analyzes a single MLP. It is listed so #45 knows it exists.
6. **Furniture as an absorber.** Upholstered seats absorb a little at 125 Hz (a university course table: about 0.15–0.20 per seat as a Sabine coefficient, secondary). Below 100 Hz furniture matters little (MALUSKI-2004, item 2.1.7). Not worth modelling.

## 7. Gaps

1. **Web access.** The fetcher couldn't reach any host, so every web source here was read through search-engine summaries only. Re-read in full before #45 closes: EBU-3276 and EBU-3276-S1 (the obstruction and symmetry wording, and whether the supplement carries the obstruction rule over), ODEON-2025 (its low-frequency α curve for an opening), CEDIA/CTA-RP22 v1.2 (it may have rules on obstructions, seat-to-seat variation or openings), the Genelec manuals from genelec.com, and the Dolby IG (to confirm it says nothing about openings or furniture).
2. **Textbooks not re-read.** The per-wall weights for damping per mode (KUTTRUFF §3.4 and ch. 5; Morse & Ingard) are **UNVERIFIED**. M4 can't be built without them. Toole's book was seen only through forum quotes. Nothing from it is cited as fact.
3. **No primary source** for: "speaker in front of a window", "subwoofer in a doorway", a speaker inside a cabinet, a measured size for the near-back-wall bass boost, or α for a door-sized opening at 20–200 Hz.
4. **Coupled rooms** have no closed form usable without the adjacent room's geometry (§2.1.4). Open-plan rooms (a missing wall) are not rectangular and stay outside ADR 0002.
5. **Hunt et al. (1939)** and **Maluski & Gibbs (2004)** were seen only through papers that cite them.
6. **Source count**: 17 primary or secondary-primary keys (books, standards, papers, manufacturer documents; most read through summaries only) and 5 community-practice keys (AVS Forum, Gearspace, Audioholics, Stereonet, and a manufacturer marketing blog). No number in this note rests on a community source alone.
